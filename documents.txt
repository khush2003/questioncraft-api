Page Content: $$( Background
Standardized English tests using human evaluation are often costly, time-consuming, and
subjective.
Automated assessment technologies with early efforts focusing on written language have
emerged, aiming for scalable and objective evaluations.
Spoken proficiency poses a significantly greater challenge due to its inherent variability.
Automated Spoken Language Assessment (ASLA) offers a scalable alternative.
Pretrained on massive text corpora, LLMs possess strong linguistic priors and can evaluate
transcripts for grammar, content, and coherence.
LLMs struggle with ASR errors and lack acoustic context.
We propose fine-tuning LLMs using QLoRA on a spoken language dataset. Our proposed
solution integrates transcripts with acoustic-prosodic features and linguistic features as text and
introduces acoustic context in LLMs. )$$

Page Content: $$( Calibration
To assess whether post-prediction calibration could improve performance, we
evaluated four methods:
Isotonic Regression: Non-parametric, monotonic mapping that preserves
prediction order while fitting to true scores.
Linear Regression: Simple linear transformation between predicted and true
scores, serving as a baseline.
Temperature Scaling: Rescales predictions via a single learned temperature
parameter, commonly used in neural network calibration.
Dirichlet Calibration: Adapted for regression by discretizing scores into bins
and applying smoothing to align predicted and empirical distributions. )$$

Page Content: $$( Conclusion
This study charts a practical path for integrating scalable LLMs into ASLA. It
demonstrates that fine-tuning on multimodal data (text + prosody) significantly
outperforms text-only baselines.
Advanced LLMs can be effectively integrated into automated CEFR assessment,
with Qwen 2.5 7B showing the strongest performance.
Combining linguistic and prosodic features, along with post-prediction calibration
(especially isotonic regression), significantly improves accuracy.
Per-score analysis reveals central-tendency bias and reduced performance at
proficiency extremes, highlighting key areas for future improvement.
Overall, the study provides a roadmap for developing more accurate, interpretable,
and scalable ASLA systems. )$$

Page Content: $$( Limitations
The study is limited by a small dataset that strongly influences model behavior.
Training and evaluation data are concentrated in intermediate proficiency levels
(B1–B2). Lower (A2) and higher (C1) proficiency levels are sparsely represented.
This uneven distribution limits the model’s ability to learn robust patterns for
underrepresented proficiency ranges. As a result, model accuracy decreases at the
lower and upper ends of the CEFR scale. )$$

Page Content: $$( Future Work
Future work could explore finetuning larger LLMs or adopting multimodal
LLMs to improve accuracy.
Acoustic-prosodic cues could be studied via ablation and interpretability
methods (e.g., SHAP) to identify influential features.
Synthetic chain-of-thought demonstrations may help models internalize
reasoning steps and improve scoring reliability.
Multi-task learning—predicting diagnostic sub-features (disfluencies,
pronunciation errors, speech rate) alongside CEFR scores—could yield
deeper insights into learner performance.
Leveraging larger, more balanced datasets or incorporating bias-mitigation
strategies could help improve model robustness, particularly at performance
extremes. )$$
